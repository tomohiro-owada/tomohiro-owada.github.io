I don't think the joy of shopping can be taken from people. Looking around shops, and who you buy from, are also part of shopping. Even if I leave the paying to AI, I don't want to hand over the choosing.

## Paying can now be entrusted

On November 13, OpenAI announced [ChatGPT Wallet](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-wallet/). It is a system that lets [Dots](/en/posts/ai-price-war-lock-in/), ChatGPT's persistent agent, complete payments on the user's behalf. It works with American Express, Mastercard, and Visa, and the card number itself is not passed to the agent or to the store. Before paying, you confirm the items, the total, and the card to use, then approve with Face ID (facial recognition) or a fingerprint. For now, it is available to ChatGPT Pro users in the United States who are 18 or older.

On the same day, Google also [connected Gemini to Google Wallet](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/gemini-app/google-wallet-gemini-app/). That one only shows spending graphs and talks about budgets; it cannot move money. One side hands over payment; the other is view-only.

My interest is in the payment side. Depending on the amount and the conditions, I would like to leave even the paying to AI.

## The fight over where shopping starts

It isn't only OpenAI and Google. On the 13th, the same day as the ChatGPT Wallet announcement, Reuters reported, based on internal documents, that Amazon is pursuing a plan called [World Product Graph](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/amazons-plan-own-worlds-product-data-fight-ai-shopping-agents-2026-11-13/) ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1033697/amazon-world-product-graph-ai-search)). It is a concept for gathering data on nearly every product in the world, including ones Amazon itself does not sell. The documents say the company would receive data from manufacturers and retailers, and also collect it independently from other retail sites.

The aim is to make Amazon the place where people start looking up what to buy. In a 2025 Morgan Stanley survey, 57% of people began product research on Google, and 16% on Amazon. Amazon also wants to use this data to improve the accuracy of its own shopping AI, Alexa for Shopping (formerly called Rufus). In the same vein, in September 2026 it blocked access from Meta's shopping agent Muse, and in 2025 it sued Perplexity to stop similar access. It does not want to hand the entry point over to another company's AI.

The internal documents also apparently include a launch date of October 1, 2027. Whether the plan will proceed as written, though, is not clear. Amazon has said that concept-stage documents may not reflect its current plans.

I think ChatGPT Wallet, Gemini, and Amazon all want to own the place where shopping starts. It is not only paying that is being contested, but the entry point itself: looking up what to buy, where to buy it, and who you buy it from. If there will still be situations where who you buy from matters, I want to keep an eye on that entry point drifting toward a single company.

## Restocking I can leave to it. Choosing, I want to do myself

For restocking consumables I always buy, it's fine to leave it to AI. It's just adding a fixed item at a fixed time. But choosing and buying, I want to do myself.

I think there will still be situations where the experience of the brand, and who you buy from, matters. If something can be chosen fully by specs, AI's judgment is fine. But for something I touch every day, like a smartphone, I want to choose it myself, including where I buy it from.

Time spent looking around shops, like window shopping, is also part of shopping. A table of prices and specs alone will not leave that time intact.

## It's hard to convey every condition

Even for the usual consumables, though, leaving it to AI is not simple. That's because it's hard to convey every condition for the thing you want to buy, with nothing left out.

I once left the comparison of Nespresso-compatible coffee capsules, up to just before the order, to AI ([an earlier post](/en/posts/capsule-expiry/)). It was the AI that found the cheapest Colombia. But the name of that option had an expiry date on it, and it was stock that would expire before it arrived. The expiry was three days after that day, and delivery was scheduled after that. I noticed on the confirmation screen, right before buying. Hidden in the cheapness was the fact that it was close to expiry.

"Buy cheap compatible capsules" had not conveyed the expiry date. AI is good at choosing by specs and price, but if you can't hand over every condition, it can end up choosing by cheapness alone.

Restocking consumables itself is not a new story. Amazon used to have the Dash Button, which let you order a fixed product with a single press. [Sales ended on February 28, 2019](https://www.engadget.com/2019-03-01-amazon-discontinues-dash-buttons.html), and [existing buttons also stopped working on August 31 of the same year](https://www.theverge.com/2019/8/1/20750814/amazon-dash-buttons-discontinued-orders-august-31st). If all you were doing was restocking the usual items, no judgment was needed; one button was enough. Even so, after that disappeared, AI is now trying to do the same thing, this time with judgment attached. You can't simply say it becomes more convenient because judgment was added. With the capsules, that judgment leaned toward cheapness, and it arrived at stock that was close to expiry.

## Why I want to stop at the confirmation screen

That's why, even if I leave restocking consumables to AI, I want to keep a place to stop at the confirmation screen, before the money moves. In that earlier purchase, I entered the card security code (CVV) myself. I leave the comparing and the operations to it, and keep only the last push for myself. Because that stopping place was there, I didn't end up buying the expired capsules.

OpenAI writes that, eventually, users will likely be able to set amount limits and categories in advance, and leave approvals in bulk. The effort will go down. But what disappears then is not only the effort of confirming. The time spent looking at the screen and checking for yourself what you are about to buy disappears with it. That time is a place to stop a mistake, and also part of the joy of shopping.

I can hand over the wallet, but I don't want to hand over the time spent looking at the screen at the end. That time is still shopping.

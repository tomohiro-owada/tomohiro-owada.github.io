I take on remote work from a Japanese company, so I earn in yen and live on Philippine pesos. That makes the yen-peso rate the number I care about most every morning. On October 22, that rate moved in a strange way.

## Rate hike day

On October 22, the BSP (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, the Philippine central bank) raised its policy rate (the benchmark interest rate a central bank sets) by 0.5 percentage points to 5.5%. Apparently it was a bigger hike than the market had expected.

Normally, raising interest rates makes a country's currency more attractive to buy. With prices rising in the Philippines right now, it should also have been a move that helps stop the peso's slide.

## The peso still hit a record low

Yet that day the peso closed at 63.52 to the dollar, a new record low. During trading it sank as far as 63.545 ([Inquirer](https://business.inquirer.net/617362/peso-extends-losses-falls-past-63-51)). It broke the record low it had set only the day before.

According to the article, there were two main reasons:

- After U.S. inflation data, expectations grew that the Fed (the U.S. central bank) would raise rates again, and investors bought dollars
- Oil prices rose because of the conflict in the Middle East, and the Philippines, which relies on imports, needs more dollars

Even a 0.5-point hike couldn't stop the strong-dollar trend.

The yen, meanwhile, went the other way and was bought. A sell-off in AI-related stocks strengthened the risk-off mood and drove demand for the yen as a safe-haven currency, and falling U.S. interest rates narrowed the gap between Japanese and U.S. rates ([FXStreet](https://www.fxstreet.com/news/japanese-yen-surges-as-risk-off-mood-drives-safe-haven-demand-202610221555)). By the ECB (European Central Bank) reference rate, October 22 came in at ¥10,000 = ₱4,044 (¥1 = ₱0.4044), the strongest the yen had been in a month ([Frankfurter/ECB](https://api.frankfurter.dev/v1/2026-10-22?from=USD&to=PHP,JPY)). A month earlier, on September 22, it was ¥10,000 = ₱3,974, so the same ¥10,000 now gets you about ₱70 more.

For someone who earns in yen and lives in pesos, a weak peso and a strong yen on the same day made it a great day to exchange money.

## But prices are rising

I can't just celebrate, though. According to the PSA (Philippine Statistics Authority), September's CPI (consumer price index) was up 7.2% year on year, and rice was up 20.3% ([PSA](https://psa.gov.ph/price-indices/cpi-ir)).

A weak peso feeds straight into the prices of anything that depends on imports.

- Meralco (the electric utility serving Metro Manila) raised its October rate to ₱15.06 per kWh. For a household using 200 kWh, that's about ₱64 more a month. Meralco itself lists the weak peso as one of the reasons for the increase ([Meralco](https://company.meralco.com.ph/news-and-advisories/higher-residential-rates-october-2026))
- LPG (bottled propane gas for home use) went up by as much as ₱190.60 per 11 kg tank in October, according to the Department of Energy ([DOE](https://doe.gov.ph/liquid-fuels-and-lpg-price-adjustments-for-6-13-october-2026))

When you get paid in yen and convert to pesos, a weak peso means more money in your pocket. But the weak peso is also pushing up prices here, and I buy rice and use electricity here too. Getting to exchange on a strong-yen day isn't something I can celebrate without reservation.

## Where the gains end up

On the day the central bank raised rates to defend its currency, the peso hit a record low, and the one who came out ahead was me, earning in yen. That's ₱70 more per ¥10,000 than a month earlier, but the weak peso is also listed among Meralco's reasons for raising its rates. For a household using 200 kWh, ₱64 of that goes straight back to Meralco as the electricity bill.
